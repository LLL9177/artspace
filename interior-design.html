Japanese Traditional

Harmony · Simplicity · Nature

Japanese Traditional

TRADITIONAL JAPANESE INTERIOR

Traditional Japanese interiors are built around simplicity, balance and a close relationship with nature. Rather than filling a room with furniture and decoration, the design gives every object and every empty space a purpose.

One of the defining features of a traditional Japanese room is the tatami floor, made from woven natural materials. Sliding shoji screens allow light to enter while dividing spaces without creating a feeling of separation. Furniture is usually low and minimal, leaving much of the room open. Natural wood, paper, bamboo and other materials create a calm atmosphere. The philosophy behind this approach is closely connected to ideas of simplicity, impermanence and harmony with nature. Instead of trying to make a room impressive through decoration, Japanese design often finds beauty in what is left out.

Art Deco

Luxury · Geometry · Elegance

Art Deco

1920s–1930s · DECORATIVE STYLE

Art Deco is known for its bold geometry, luxurious materials and carefully arranged decoration. It combines modern shapes with an unmistakable sense of glamour.

Art Deco became popular during the 1920s and 1930s. Its name comes from the 1925 International Exhibition of Modern Decorative and Industrial Arts in Paris. Interiors often feature symmetrical arrangements, geometric patterns, polished wood, glass, mirrors and metallic details. Designers such as Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann helped define the style through elegant furniture and exceptional craftsmanship. Art Deco embraced modern life and technological progress, but it did so through rich materials and decorative forms. Its goal was not to hide luxury, but to make it part of everyday surroundings.

Bauhaus

Function · Simplicity · Modernity

Bauhaus

1919–1933 · MODERNIST DESIGN

Bauhaus design follows a simple principle: form should follow function. It removes unnecessary decoration and focuses on useful objects, clean lines and practical spaces.

The Bauhaus school was founded in Germany in 1919 by architect Walter Gropius. It brought together art, architecture and craftsmanship. Bauhaus interiors emphasize functionality, geometric forms and the honest use of materials. Furniture is often simple, practical and designed for mass production. The movement rejected the idea that beauty required excessive decoration. Instead, it argued that a well-designed object could be beautiful precisely because it worked so well. Although the original school closed in 1933, its ideas had a lasting influence on modern architecture, furniture and interior design.

Victorian

Ornamentation · Richness · Detail

Victorian

1837–1901 · HISTORIC STYLE

Victorian interiors are often associated with rich colours, elaborate furniture, patterned fabrics and decorative details. They reflect an era in which the home was also a statement of taste and status.

Victorian interior design developed during the reign of Queen Victoria in Britain, from 1837 to 1901. The period saw major changes in manufacturing, trade and the availability of household goods. As a result, decorative furniture and ornaments became increasingly accessible to the growing middle class. Typical interiors used dark wood, detailed upholstery, heavy curtains, patterned wallpaper and elaborate furniture. Unlike minimalist approaches, Victorian design often celebrated abundance. Rooms were filled with objects, textures and personal possessions, creating spaces that felt expressive and lived in.

Minimalism

Space · Balance · Restraint

Minimalism

20TH CENTURY · MODERN DESIGN

Minimalist interiors focus on what is essential. Clean surfaces, simple forms, natural light and carefully selected objects create spaces that feel open and peaceful.

Minimalism in interior design developed alongside broader minimalist movements in art and architecture during the twentieth century. It emphasizes simplicity, functionality and the careful use of space. Furniture is often reduced to essential forms, while decoration is kept to a minimum. Neutral colours, natural materials and generous amounts of light are common features. However, minimalism is not simply about owning fewer things. It is about making deliberate choices and giving each element a reason to exist. A room does not need to be full to feel complete.
