Five remarkable works that shaped the history of sculpture.

David

Michelangelo · 1501–1504

David

Michelangelo · 1501–1504

David is one of the most celebrated sculptures of the Renaissance. It depicts the biblical hero David before his battle with Goliath. Rather than showing the moment of victory, Michelangelo captures David in a state of concentration and readiness.

Michelangelo began working on David in 1501, using a massive block of marble that had previously been abandoned by other sculptors. The statue stands over five metres tall and demonstrates an extraordinary understanding of human anatomy. Its proportions, detailed muscles and intense expression reflect Renaissance ideals of beauty, balance and human potential. The sculpture originally stood in front of the Palazzo Vecchio in Florence, where it became a symbol of the city's independence and civic strength.

The Thinker

Auguste Rodin · 1880–1904

The Thinker

Auguste Rodin · 1880–1904

The Thinker is one of the most recognizable sculptures in modern art. It portrays a seated man leaning forward, resting his chin on his hand, apparently lost in deep thought. The sculpture represents human reflection, creativity and intellectual struggle.

The figure was originally conceived as part of Rodin's monumental work The Gates of Hell, inspired by Dante's Divine Comedy. It was initially intended to represent Dante himself, observing the suffering of the figures below him. Rodin later developed it into an independent sculpture. Its powerful pose and muscular body combine physical strength with intense intellectual activity. Numerous casts of the sculpture exist in museums around the world.

Venus de Milo

Alexandros of Antioch · c. 130–100 BCE

Venus de Milo

Traditionally attributed to Alexandros of Antioch · c. 130–100 BCE

The Venus de Milo is an ancient Greek sculpture depicting Aphrodite, the goddess of love and beauty. Famous for its missing arms, the statue is admired for its elegant proportions, graceful pose and classical beauty.

The sculpture was discovered in 1820 on the Greek island of Milos. It was subsequently acquired by France and became one of the Louvre's most famous works. Although its creator is traditionally identified as Alexandros of Antioch, the attribution is not certain. The statue originally had arms, but they were missing when it was discovered. Its mysterious appearance and balanced composition have made it an enduring symbol of ancient Greek art.

The Kiss

Auguste Rodin · 1882

The Kiss

Auguste Rodin · 1882

The Kiss depicts two lovers embracing in a moment of intimacy. Created by Auguste Rodin, it explores themes of love, passion and human connection. Its flowing composition gives the figures a sense of movement and emotion.

The sculpture was originally designed as part of Rodin's Gates of Hell, where the figures represented the tragic lovers Paolo and Francesca from Dante's Divine Comedy. Rodin later separated the composition from the larger work. Unlike many traditional sculptures that emphasize idealized poses, The Kiss focuses on natural movement and emotional expression. Its smooth marble surface and carefully modelled bodies create a striking contrast between physical form and intimate feeling.

Great Sphinx of Giza

Ancient Egypt · c. 2500 BCE

Great Sphinx of Giza

Ancient Egyptian sculptors · c. 2500 BCE

The Great Sphinx of Giza is one of the oldest and largest monumental sculptures in the world. It combines the body of a lion with the head of a human, symbolizing strength, wisdom and royal authority.

The Great Sphinx is believed to have been created during the reign of Pharaoh Khafre, around 2500 BCE, although some details of its history remain debated. It was carved directly from a natural limestone formation rather than assembled from separate pieces. The monument is approximately 73 metres long and 20 metres high. Over the centuries, it has suffered from erosion and damage, yet it remains one of the most recognizable monuments of ancient Egyptian civilization.
